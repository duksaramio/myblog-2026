Fifty-five stories cleared 200 points on the first five pages tonight, twenty-nine of them new. That is the biggest new-story count in a week, and it is almost entirely down to one filing.

Anthropic's S-1 is on the front page at 1,253 points with 1,220 comments, and it is the only document in this roundup that anybody can check line by line. Revenue of $16.3 billion in six months. Compute and infrastructure at $8.9 billion in the same six months. A $120.5 billion commitment to a company that was called xAI a few months ago. A net loss of $80.7 billion, most of it mark-to-market noise on convertible notes. And the single most interesting line is an accounting policy change: in 2025 Anthropic stopped reporting gross profit.

Everything else on the page tonight is either a smaller version of that argument — cheap models, on-prem models, a CEO saying 90% of the performance at 10% of the price — or a clean break from it. A woman who has given away 343 million books. A Minecraft Switzerland you can walk across. A journalist being quoted $2.5 million for a police file. The page is unusually good tonight. Start with the filing.

## Anthropic S-1 — 1,253 points

**1,253 points** · 1,220 comments · [sec.gov](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50242939)

Filed this morning, Nasdaq symbol ANT, offered through a public benefit corporation with a Long-Term Benefit Trust holding Class T shares that elect a majority of the board, plus a founder-held Class F share. All of that governance detail is in the first four pages and all of it is a distraction from the numbers, which are the reason this is the best story of the week.

Revenue: $41 million in 2023, $386 million in 2024, $4.585 billion in 2025, and $16.286 billion in the six months to June 30, 2026, against $1.163 billion in the same period last year. Q2 2026 alone was $11.6 billion. Operating loss of $693 million for the half, operating margin of positive 8% in Q2, adjusted operating income of $1.6 billion for the half. Revenue per employee on a trailing-twelve-month basis went from $1.4 million to $6.6 million while headcount went from 940 at the end of 2024 to about 5,300 now. If those numbers are what they look like, this is the fastest revenue ramp in the history of enterprise software and the margin structure of a company that has already won.

Then you read the expense lines. Compute and infrastructure was $8.945 billion in six months — 55% of revenue — against $2.334 billion in the year-ago half. Sales, marketing and partnerships was $3.168 billion, 19% of revenue, up from $120 million, and $2.7 billion of that increase is "fees paid to channel partners." That is the vig: the clouds Anthropic also owes compute money to are taking a cut of the revenue they route to it. Personnel ran $3.152 billion, of which $2.2 billion was stock-based compensation in the half. Other operating and administrative, $1.714 billion, does not reconcile with anything on the page in a way a reader can audit.

The 2025 change is the tell. From the filing: "During the year ended December 31, 2025, the Company changed its presentation of operating expenses from a functional classification under which cost of revenue, gross profit (loss), research and development, sales and marketing, and general and administrative were presented as separate line items, to a classification based on the nature of expenses." Read that again. A company heading into the largest technology IPO in history stopped disclosing gross profit in the year before it filed. The functional view survived in the notes — substantially all cost of revenue is compute and infrastructure for customer-serving workloads — but the headline statements no longer let you compute a gross margin without doing the work yourself. When a vendor's own audited numbers stop showing the one ratio that defines a software business, that is not a formatting preference.

The commitments are the other half of the story. Total indebtedness of $6.2 billion, all convertible notes to Amazon through AWS, plus the ability to issue up to $42 billion more in convertibles to Broadcom for a March 2026 equipment lease on custom accelerators. Then there is SpaceXAI, formerly xAI: agreements entered and amended between May and October 2026 for NVIDIA-powered compute, "an aggregate of up to $120.5 billion over terms extending into 2029, consisting of approximately $3.1 billion in the aggregate per month once all capacity has been delivered, which we expect in December 2026." Three point one billion dollars a month, and the initial non-cancelable periods end in January and March 2027 — after which either side can walk on 90 days' notice. A $120 billion monthly-billing compute deal with an early exit in eight weeks is not a commitment, it is a call option on someone else's capex.

The blank is the best part. The aggregate committed spend figure across all purchase commitments and leases, "substantially all of which is related to compute and infrastructure," with roughly $X before 2030 and the rest between 2031 and 2045, is a literal `$` followed by blank space. Subject to completion. The largest line item in the largest tech IPO ever filed is a placeholder, and no commenter noticed because the commenters were too busy arguing about valuation.

Which they do, at length, and mostly badly. The strongest objection in the thread is the simplest one: at a rumored valuation north of a trillion dollars, every dollar of future growth is already priced, so the only way to make money on the trade is to be right about a market that does not exist yet. The strongest defense is that the demand is real and constrained by capacity — the $2.08 of revenue per dollar of compute figure circulating in comments, up from $0.40 in Q1 2025, is the single number that would justify the build-out if it holds. It is not in the filing in that form, and I could not verify it there; treat it as a commenter's arithmetic.

One detail that deserves more attention than it got: the lock-up. Insiders can sell starting day one, then at 27, 48, 62 and 76 days after the debut, instead of the standard 180. That is a schedule designed by people who expect the first print to be the high one. Also worth noting for anyone tracking corporate filings as a genre: the Bartz settlement cost $1.5 billion in 2025, one third of that year's revenue, and appears as its own operating expense line. Copyright is a cost of goods here, not a tail risk.

The company's own risk language is more honest than its boosters. "We do not expect our revenue will continue to grow at comparable rates, and we cannot assure you that it will continue to grow at all." Fine. What no risk factor addresses is the thing the expense table says out loud: this is a business with a 55% compute bill, a 19% channel bill, and a customer base where the top five accounts are a quarter of revenue and "many of our top customers are not subject to long-term contracts with us." The margins arrive if the compute gets cheaper and the channels get disintermediated. Both of those are the stated strategy of the two companies that also own the channels.

## Dolly Parton's Imagination Library — 666 points

**666 points** · 107 comments · [imaginationlibrary.com](https://imaginationlibrary.com/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50234474)

The front page found a website that works. Dolly Parton's Imagination Library mails a free age-appropriate book every month to any child from birth to age five, in the US, Canada, the UK, Australia and Ireland, and has done since 1995 — 31 years, 3,197,250 children registered, 343,596,903 books given away. Started in her home county in east Tennessee because her father could not read or write. Funding is split between Parton and local community partners, which is why the program is administered county by county rather than nationally.

The comments are the usual "how is this not bigger news" thread, and the honest answer is that it is not news because it is not a claim. There is no benchmark, no valuation, no roadmap. It is one organization, one logistics contract, and a number that goes up. Against everything else on tonight's page it reads as an accusation.

Two things worth knowing before you sign a kid up. The waitlists are geographic — coverage depends on whether a local affiliate sponsors your postal code, and large parts of the US are not covered. And the program's own research page is where the interesting material lives: the international studies on early literacy are less spectacular than the topline counters and more useful, because they measure whether the books get read rather than whether they get mailed.

## Oregon Police Are Charging a Local News Outlet $2.5M for Records About One Cop — 508 points

**508 points** · 143 comments · [theintercept.com](https://theintercept.com/2026/10/01/oregon-police-misconduct-public-records-cost-fee/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50234232)

Double Sided Media in Eugene asked for records about officer Justin Peckels, who has been the subject of complaints including punching a resident during a traffic stop hard enough to break his nose. The Eugene Police Department's estimate: 44,816 hours of staff time, $2,484,599.04. A parallel request about a former officer, Eric Klinko, was quoted at $195,869.52. Total for two officers' files: about $2.7 million.

The justification is the story. The department says it cannot narrow the custodians it would have to search, so it must search the records of every city employee past and present — parks and recreation included — manually, one by one. Either Peckels has the largest disciplinary file in the Western world or the records system is configured so that compliance is unaffordable. The city's letter is explicit that staff will "continue processing" once payment arrives, and that any shortfall becomes a further bill before release.

Read as a procurement problem, it is almost defensible: a badly indexed document store and a request scoped to "any record mentioning this person between 2018 and 2026" genuinely is a lot of human hours. Read as an incentive problem, it is a price list for the right to know. Oregon's public records law has no fee cap and no deemed-approval mechanism, so the only remedy is litigation by a two-person newsroom against a city legal budget. This is the same failure mode as discovery costs in civil litigation, applied to journalism, and it works precisely because it is legal.

## Birdwatching rewires the brain and slows cognitive decline — 439 points

**439 points** · 179 comments · [bbc.com](https://www.bbc.com/future/article/20261019-birdwatching-rewires-the-brain-and-slows-cognitive-decline) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50215997)

The neuroscience is real, and narrower than the headline. Erik Wing's group at Baycrest in Toronto scanned expert birders and novices while they identified birds, and found expert brains wired differently in attention, visual perception and visual recognition regions, with more activity in those regions specifically when identifying unfamiliar birds. Experts also showed higher measures of network organisation — a proxy for cognitive health that normally declines with age — in some of the same regions.

That is correlational, and the article says so. The structural differences could be the cause or the effect of expertise; nobody has run the intervention. The clinical half is more solid and less headline-friendly: Age UK runs birdwatching groups for dementia patients in the West Midlands, and the reported outcomes are things like a withdrawn 78-year-old laughing at ducks. That is not a cognitive endpoint, it is a mood endpoint, and mood is what the carers actually need.

The generalisable claim — expertise in a demanding perceptual domain preserves brain organisation — is not new and not about birds. It worked for taxi drivers and musicians first. What is new here is an unusually well-matched control group (novices doing the same task) and a task with a real expertise gradient. If you want the honest version: learn something hard that involves looking closely at the world, and keep doing it. The bird is incidental.

**405 points** · 314 comments · [theregister.com](https://www.theregister.com/systems/2026/10/22/ibm-ceo-says-open-weight-models-are-now-irresistibly-good-and-cheap-on-prem/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50238088)

## OpenAI and Anthropic cut their LLM prices by up to 80% within a few months — 402 points

**402 points** · 406 comments · [minimaxir.com](https://minimaxir.com/2026/10/cheap-llms/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50236102)

His argument about why is the good part: nobody cuts prices 80% without an externality. The skepticism the post does not apply to itself: "the models do not appear to have cut any corners" is the author's personal judgement across a handful of models, and the timeline is built from vendor announcements, which are the least reliable source for capability claims in the industry. But the price series is real, it is auditable, and it is the best argument on tonight's page for why Anthropic's 55% compute line might compress. It is also the best argument for why its revenue might not hold.

## Broccoli2Shell: Brotli decoder RCE in Node.js, Python and more — 399 points

**399 points** · 214 comments · [broccoli2shell.com](https://broccoli2shell.com/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50219177)

Out-of-bounds write in the Brotli decoder, versions 1.0.3 through 1.2.0, CVSS 9.2, remote code execution. Fixed in 1.0.10, 1.1.1 and 1.2.1; Node 26.12.1, 24.22.1 and 22.23.4 carry the fix; Node 25, 23, 20 and older get nothing because those lines are end of life. Python's `Brotli` and `brotlicffi` are patched at 1.2.1 and 1.2.1.0.

The exposure surface is what makes this worth reading rather than filing. Node's built-in `fetch()` decompresses `Content-Encoding: br` responses automatically, and so do `requests`/`urllib3` and `aiohttp` when a Brotli package is installed — meaning any server you talk to can send the payload. Express 5.0.0-5.3.0 and body-parser 2.0.0-2.3.0 inflate Brotli request bodies in `raw()` and `json()`, which turns an obscure feature into an unauthenticated inbound path. And installing the update is not enough: a running process keeps the old decoder until it restarts, and a container keeps it until you rebuild on a patched base.

The comment thread contains the two corrections that matter. The fix appears to have landed in Google's source in February without anyone recognising it as a security issue, so the vulnerability sat patched-but-unreleased for eight months — and one commenter asks whether an AI automation pipeline noticed the diff and nobody was at the wheel. The other is the irony: Google declined to ship JPEG XL in Chrome partly because a new image decoder enlarges the attack surface, while having shipped Brotli, which every HTTP client now decodes by default.

## Overgrown, when plants invade your abandoned tabs — 394 points

**394 points** · 131 comments · [charliegerard.dev](https://charliegerard.dev/blog/overgrown-extension/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50197108)

A Chrome extension where each tab has a neglect clock that runs only while the tab is out of view. Leave one long enough and plants grow in from the edges; past 5% coverage the page behind the vines stops accepting clicks, selection and drags. You clear it by dragging the mouse across the growth — machete, chainsaw or flamethrower, your choice — and when you get it back under 5% the remainder falls off. Then it starts again.

Charlie Gerard built it as a creative exercise around the word "overgrowth," and the reason it works is that it makes an abstract cost physical. The engineering detail worth stealing is the neglect clock: visibility-gated timers mean the mechanic costs nothing while you are actually working, which is the opposite of every productivity extension that fights for attention.

## AI use leads UC Berkeley math professor to see 'no point' in grading homework — 231 points

**231 points** · 327 comments · [dailycal.org](https://www.dailycal.org/news/campus/academics/ai-use-leads-uc-berkeley-math-professor-to-see-no-point-in-grading-homework/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50233304)

Richard Borcherds, Fields medalist, no longer grades homework for correctness. He grades on completion, admits it is "not a very good system," and has no alternative — students using AI get consistently top scores, so paying readers to check answers is now pointless work. He says if AI keeps improving at the current rate there will be nothing human mathematicians can do better within a few years, that he does not know what to advise students, and that he asked ChatGPT which also did not know.

The comment ratio here — 327 comments on 231 points — tells you this hit a nerve the other AI stories did not. The thread splits cleanly. One camp argues homework was always a proxy for learning and that the proxy has now failed, so the answer is assessment that happens in a room without a network connection. The other camp argues that completion grading is an abdication dressed as realism, and that a Fields medalist's despair is not a policy.

Both sides skip the thing Borcherds himself says and then drops: campus tried Turnitin and Rumi and found neither adequate to standardise. That is the actual state of the art — detection does not work, so institutions are choosing between unsupervised take-home work they cannot verify and supervised work they cannot scale. His comparison to paper journals, which have published for twenty years while being "utterly pointless," is the most honest sentence in the piece, because it describes what will probably happen: the ritual continues, the credential inflates, and the people who care find another way to learn.

## Moderna and Merck say their mRNA cancer vaccine cut death, recurrence by 42% — 203 points

**203 points** · 52 comments · [arstechnica.com](https://arstechnica.com/health/2026/10/personalized-mrna-cancer-vaccine-cut-melanoma-death-recurrence-by-42/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50235217)

The topline from INTERPATH-001, released early after an oncology outlet broke the ESMO embargo: intismeran (mRNA-4157) plus Keytruda reduced the risk of recurrence or death by 42% versus Keytruda plus placebo in resected stage IIB-IV melanoma, 1,137 patients randomised 2:1, two-year follow-up. Absolute numbers: 82% recurrence-free versus 70%. Distant metastasis-free survival: 38% risk reduction, 88.5% versus 80%.

Three things to hold onto before the press cycle gets going. First, the hazard ratio is not the effect size. A 42% reduction on a 30% event rate at two years is roughly nine percentage points of absolute benefit per hundred patients treated, and that is the number that matters for a payer, a guideline committee, and a patient deciding whether to take an individualized manufacturing slot that requires tumour sequencing, a bespoke mRNA synthesis run, and a lead time measured in weeks.

Second, the effect shrank from Phase 2. KEYNOTE-942 reported 49% RFS and 59% DMFS reduction at five years with 157 patients; Phase 3 gives 44% and 38% with 1,137. That is the normal direction for a phase 3 and it is worth remembering the next time someone quotes the phase 2 numbers as settled fact.

Third, and this is the one the coverage will not mention: this is an adjuvant trial with a Keytruda backbone, so the incremental value is entirely on top of the standard of care that Merck already sells. Merck is running the trial that extends the patent life of its own franchise, and the pricing question — individualized neoantigen manufacturing, not a vial off a shelf — has no answer in the release. The FDA has no established pathway for a per-patient manufactured cancer vaccine at scale, and the release says only that the companies are "engaging with regulatory authorities."

None of that makes the result small. A 9-point absolute improvement in recurrence-free survival in resected high-risk melanoma is a genuine advance, the first Phase 3 win for individualized mRNA cancer therapy, and the strongest evidence yet that the platform generalises beyond infectious disease. It is also, on tonight's page, the only story where the gap between what was measured and what will be claimed is the whole story.

## Throughline

**Tonight the front page has a set of audited numbers on it, and everything else is a claim about the same numbers.** Anthropic's S-1 says a company can generate $16.3 billion in six months and still spend 55% of it on compute and 19% of it on the channel partners who also sell it the compute. Krishna says the same thing from IBM's side: 90% of the performance at 10% of the price is where the enterprise market is going, and he is happy to sell you the racks. Max Woolf's price timeline shows the labs cutting prices 75-80% in four months because inference is commoditising faster than capability is differentiating. GPT-6.1 Terra is the product expression of that: not the best model, the cheapest good-enough one, priced at a tenth of the frontier. Four stories, one mechanism, and the mechanism is that the thing everyone is building is getting cheap while the things you need to build it — wafers, power, channels — are not.

**The second theme is the cost of being allowed to check.** A newsroom is quoted $2.5 million for a police file because the records system makes compliance expensive. A court cannot audit a recommendation algorithm because the company says the mechanism is too complex to describe. Researchers cannot grade homework because detection tools failed standardisation. Voters cannot verify the campaign they just voted on. None of these are censorship — they are all the same move: making verification cost more than the person asking can afford. The S-1 is the exception that proves the rule, because a company with no choice but to tell the truth under penalty of law produces 492,000 words that answer almost every question worth asking, including the one it accidentally answers by leaving a dollar sign blank.

**And third: the page keeps rewarding things that can be verified rather than things that can be marketed.** A Minecraft Switzerland built from open geodata, exactly 4,634 blocks high, because swisstopo published the terrain. A birdwatching study with a matched novice control. A Brotli bug with version numbers and a fix list. A retired engineer saying the last 18 months at Microsoft were full of reorgs. 343 million books sent to children, which is not a benchmark but is not a claim either. The most-quoted number on the page will be 42%, from a Phase 3 trial where the phase 2 number was 49% and the absolute difference was 12 points. The least-quoted number will be $3.1 billion a month, which is what Anthropic has agreed to pay for compute starting in December.
