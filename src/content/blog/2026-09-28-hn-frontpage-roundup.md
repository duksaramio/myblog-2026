## When did Google get so weird? — 1,777 points

**1,777 points** · 981 comments · [sancho.bearblog.dev](https://sancho.bearblog.dev/google-weird/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49870367)

The author searched "hes never coming over dario" — a mid-2010s Philadelphia 76ers meme about Dario Saric, drafted in 2014 while still under contract in Turkey — hoping to find old tweets. What he got was an AI Overview that decided he had been abandoned by a man named Dario and offered him sympathy, opening with a sentence explaining that Google is "a search engine which does not have human emotions." The links he actually wanted were a few hundred pixels below the consoling robot.

The mechanism is the interesting part, and an HN commenter supplied it: the AI layer treats queries as prose, not as strings to match, so a first-person or imperative phrase gets read as a statement about the user rather than a pattern to hunt for. Google patches individual instances of this and new ones keep appearing — there is an entire Know Your Meme genre of "make the AI overview help with an absurd scenario." That's not a bug queue, it's an architecture.

Two things make this the top story. First, the mismatch is now structural rather than embarrassing: "organize the world's information" and "be an empathetic listener" are different products, and Google is running both in one box. Second, the score itself is the datapoint. A 600-word complaint about the search box outranked the launch of GPT-6 Sol and Luna, which has been pinned near the top all week and moved two points in the last forty-eight hours. HN's most cynical reading in the thread — that Google is monetizing loneliness and the lonely are buying — is probably right and doesn't change what the product is failing to do: return a link.

## Owed a billion dollars in Nvidia stock — 1,033 points

**1,033 points** · 432 comments · [colo.to](https://colo.to/nvidia-stock-narrative.html) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49872723)

Eric Gullichsen was invited onto Nvidia's Technical Advisory Board in 1993 after a meeting with Jensen Huang, Curtis Priem and Chris Malachowsky on his houseboat in Sausalito — he had a fast biquadratic texture-mapping implementation that Priem wanted for the NV1. He was granted 25,000 options on a written schedule that vested everything one year from grant date. Then Microsoft shipped DirectX with triangles only, the NV1's quadratic mapping became a liability, Nvidia cut most of its staff, and in April 1996 the CFO wrote to say 15,625 shares had vested and must be exercised. He exercised them and forgot about it.

The number 15,625 is exactly 62.5% — the ten-quarter mark of a four-year schedule, not the one-year schedule the signed agreement specified. He found the discrepancy in 2024 while digging through old papers after a day of watching Nvidia coverage, hired Allan Steyer and Chris Burke, and spent a year exchanging letters. Nvidia never disputed the authenticity of the agreement; it disputed whether he was still allowed to complain about it. With Nvidia's cumulative 480x splits, the missing 9,375 shares are about 4.5 million today. Cooley's answer, by his own account, amounted to "so sue us," and his attorneys concluded the statute of limitations would have killed it at the motion-to-dismiss stage.

Read it as a cautionary tale about what a contract is worth once enough time has passed that enforcing it becomes expensive. The HN thread's strongest reaction was the simplest one: why does a shareholder have to keep reasserting rights to stock they already own? The practical suggestion — sell the claim to a firm that funds litigation — is what a rational plaintiff does, and it would also convert a 32-year grievance into a smaller settlement. He chose not to, which is the whole point of the piece.

## Ember-1 — 572 points

**572 points** · 244 comments · [Fireworks AI](https://fireworks.ai/blog/ember-1) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49868830)

Fireworks Research released Ember-1: a specialized model built on Kimi K3 that delivers K3's quality with 40% fewer tokens. The diagnosis is specific and correct — reasoning models spend more than 90% of generated tokens on internal reasoning rather than answers, which is merely expensive on one request and quadratic across a multi-turn agentic session, because every turn replays all prior reasoning back into context to be re-read and re-billed. Turning down K3's reasoning effort gave up too much quality, so they trained the model to reason more efficiently instead, targeting the parts worth keeping: revisiting an assumption, responding to feedback, escaping unproductive loops. 50-plus training experiments, 200-plus evaluations, all run on Fireworks' own serverless training (the platform is the real pitch — no GPU provisioning, pay-per-experiment). Validation was on their own Specialized Intelligence Index, public benchmarks, customer A/B tests, and internal dogfooding, with the striking claim that their own developers didn't notice the difference.

Skeptical reading: the headline metric is tokens, not capability, and the only artifact that would settle it is a Pareto chart with quality on the other axis. HN asked exactly that — what gets lost in the compression, and is it "super awesome at Golang before and now kind of sucks" — and got no bar chart in reply. The other thing nobody in the thread said out loud: Ember-1 is a re-cut of someone else's weights, and the class of product it belongs to has a licensing question attached to it that its customers will discover before its lawyers do. The most interesting sentence in the post is buried in the middle: the reason K3 is expensive is a design choice, and the cheapest fix in the world is training the length out of it.

## Self-Hosting on the Dark Web — 334 points

**334 points** · 106 comments · [david.alvarezrosa.com](https://david.alvarezrosa.com/posts/self-hosting-on-the-dark-web/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49870295)

A clean how-to for putting a personal site on Tor: a `HiddenServiceDir` and `HiddenServicePort 80 127.0.0.1:8080` in `torrc`, an nginx block with no TLS, no HTTP/2 and no QUIC because Tor speaks plain TCP and provides its own encryption, and a build detail most people get wrong — a static site bakes absolute URLs, so you build a second copy with the onion address as the base URL and rsync each to its own web root. The pipeline does it per push so clearnet and Tor stay in sync.

The HN thread is more useful than the post about why anyone would do this. The draw for most people isn't anonymity; it's that you never touch a public IP or a certificate, which is the difference between having a server and not having one when your ISP has you behind CGNAT. The costs are also spelled out honestly: onion v3 addresses can't be said out loud, the latency makes anything chatty feel broken, so static pages only, and uptime is invisible — nobody tells you a hidden service is down until someone mentions it in passing. Own-your-stack story number one of four on today's page.

## Don't couple your Go code to GitHub — 317 points

**317 points** · 169 comments · [iain.rocks](https://iain.rocks/blog/dont-couple-your-go-code-to-github) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49868404)

Go namespaces code by its fetch location, so `import "github.com/you/thing"` hardwires the forge into every downstream file — which means moving hosting requires editing other people's source files, which means nobody moves. The author's illustration is a company paying for GitLab, GitHub and Azure DevOps simultaneously because migrating was "too much overhead," i.e. paying three times rather than once. His fix is the boring one: a vanity import path on a domain you control, which is what go.uber.org and go.mongodb.org already do. Then you can point the domain wherever you want and nobody's imports change.

HN's counterargument is the one to take seriously: a custom domain depends on one developer continuing to renew a registration, whereas GitHub is plausibly forever, so the "unportable" version may outlive the "portable" one. That's true and it answers a different question. Vanity paths aren't about uptime, they're about not needing anyone's permission to move — and the Go ecosystem's version of that permission slip is currently a git forge in another company's product line.

## In an $80 motel room, a discovery to shed light on the origins of life — 290 points

**290 points** · 110 comments · [NYT](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/26/science/motel-science-discovery.html) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49866951)

Julia Van Etten, an evolutionary biologist who joined the University of Maryland this year, sampled a roadside marsh in North Carolina's Outer Banks and worked the samples out of an $80 room in a Nags Head motel with a $300 hobby microscope and a $198 camera. She found two undescribed species of *Paulinella*, a photosynthetic amoeba with silica scales — *P. marae* and *P. murrayi*, published in the *Journal of Phycology* on September 4, bringing the known total of photosynthetic Paulinella species from four to six. The methods are the best part: cells isolated with pulled Pasteur pipettes and tools made by supergluing toothbrush bristles onto coloured pencils, because Paulinella is rare enough that a single drop of water usually contains none.

Why the genus matters: primary endosymbiosis — one cell swallowing another and keeping it as an organelle — happened about 1.5 billion years ago and produced essentially all complex photosynthetic life. Paulinella did it independently about 100 million years ago. Because that second event is comparatively recent, it's the only readable-in-detail example of a process that's otherwise inferred from a billion-year-old outcome. The team also compared chromatophore and mitochondrial genomes and found conserved gene content with rearrangements, inversions, and a discrepancy between nuclear and organelle phylogenies — which is a problem worth publishing, not a footnote.

Two caveats the thread raised fairly. The NYT frames the motel as a workaround for the Trump administration's federal science funding freezes, but never quite establishes that link, and Van Etten's low-cost microscope project predates it — the piece implies more causation than it demonstrates. And the finding everyone will miss: the paper's own claim is that Paulinella is probably globally distributed and badly under-sampled, based partly on records from hobbyists and naturalists. The lesson isn't "amateurs can do biology." It's that a whole research program was sitting in ordinary brackish water while the literature described four species.

## Parley: Federated, decentralised chat that speaks plain IRC — 256 points

**256 points** · 130 comments · [git.mills.io](https://git.mills.io/prologic/parley) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49875913)

James Mills' Parley is a chat network with no centre. Everyone runs a small instance for their own domain; instances find each other through DNS and well-known identity documents, exchange ed25519-signed JSON over HTTPS POSTs to `/inbox`, and present the resulting federation to ordinary IRC clients — irssi, WeeChat, Textual, mIRC — with no plugins. Identity maps onto IRC semantics rather than sitting on top of them, so `alice@foo.com` appears as `alice!alice@foo.com`, NAMES/WHO/WHOIS all agree, and `/whois alice@foo.com` consults her instance's WKID document. Modern IRCv3 features are relayed: `server-time`, `message-tags`, `echo-message`, `multi-prefix`, `setname`, typing indicators via `TAGMSG`, and `draft/multiline` so a pasted paragraph is one message. Go, MIT, container image, docker-compose example, and a two-instance local quickstart that needs no DNS.

HN's split was predictable and both halves are right. Half the thread called it the thing they've wanted for weeks — "it's like email, just for IM" — and the other half correctly identified it as XMPP with IRC instead of XML, which is a compliment about the design and a warning about the sequel. XMPP federated successfully and spam nearly killed it; Parley's model is explicitly open, meaning any instance whose signature checks out can talk to you, and there's no load-bearing answer in the README to unsolicited inbound. The IRC world's historical spam tools — network-level oper bans, K-lines — have no equivalent when the network is consensus. Still the first federated chat design in years that doesn't ask users to change clients, which is most of why federated chat keeps failing.

## Pirating the Pirates — 253 points

**253 points** · 100 comments · [MUBI Notebook](https://mubi.com/en/notebook/posts/pirating-the-pirates) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49880036)

A long feature on film preservation as an illegal act, told through the author's own project. In 2011 he and a roommate tried to undo MGM's early-2000s reworking of *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly*: scenes Leone cut were reinstated (161 → 179 minutes), Eastwood and Wallach dubbed the missing dialogue, a soundalike replaced the late Lee Van Cleef, and the mono track was rebuilt as surround with modern-sounding gunshots. Their first attempt failed on source material. Eight years later, with a better Blu-ray for picture and a LaserDisc for the untouched mono, plus two careers' worth of new skill, they assembled a version good enough that a repertory cinema programmer booked MGM's official DCP and swapped theirs in for a public screening.

The interviews with Spencer Draper and Arrow Films' James Flower are where the structural argument lives. Revisionism isn't new (Chaplin re-cut his silents; Wong Kar-wai regraded his catalogue in 2021 with a Heraclitus quote as justification; one director Arrow worked with refused to let a historically accurate version ship alongside his degrained, oversaturated one — "for me, he was vandalizing his own work"). Preservation by fans isn't new either: *Nosferatu* survives because prints escaped German jurisdiction after a 1925 copyright suit, and Kevin Brownlow's *Napoléon* reconstruction started as an illicit teenage hobby. What's new is scale — Harmy's Despecialized editions, Team Negative1's Project 4K77, with dozens of audio tracks and a file over 100 GB, which is impossible on a disc and unviable through legitimate channels. And the legal frame is blunt: under DMCA §1201, breaking a Blu-ray's lock is a federal violation regardless of intent, and the statute draws no line between mass distribution for profit and preserving the historical record. Fans can ship variants because they answer to nobody; a label with a budget, a licence and a rights holder cannot.

The community's self-imposed rules are the most interesting artifact here — own a physical copy of at least one version, take no money, don't repost to public sites. That's a parallel legal system for a practice the actual one has criminalized, and it's being maintained by people who describe themselves as permanently one cease-and-desist away from trouble. One correction the thread made properly: a "remux" is a bit-for-bit copy of disc data into a new container, and what the article actually describes — stitching the best video with the best audio across releases — is a hybrid remux.

## The state of SIMD in Rust in 2026 — 203 points

**203 points** · 60 comments · [shnatsel.github.io](https://shnatsel.github.io/state-of-simd-rust-2026/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49844629)

The annual survey, this year written by someone who became a maintainer of Fearless SIMD after writing the last one — a conflict of interest handled by disclosure and by circulating the draft to the authors of `std::simd`, `wide`, `pulp` and `macerator` before publishing. The setup is the x86 problem: a 64-bit CPU is only guaranteed SSE2, so using AVX2 or AVX-512 in a distributed binary means function multiversioning — compiling the same function several times and dispatching at runtime. ARM mandated NEON in 64-bit and hasn't given much since; WebAssembly requires two builds and a JavaScript check. Then the three programming models: auto-vectorization (free and unreliable, degrading as functions get complicated), portable SIMD abstractions, and platform intrinsics (fast, unportable, cfg-gated per architecture).

The thread's hot take is correct and worth keeping: there is no portable SIMD — what the portable libraries provide is portable auto-vectorization, and real "portable performance" is a property of the algorithm, not the API, with manual per-platform work still required when it matters. The institutional story is that Rust's SIMD story is being carried by third-party crates because `std::simd` has been six months from stable for years. Sixty comments on 203 points suggests a post people are bookmarking rather than arguing about.

## Throughline

**First: nothing on today's front page was built from scratch, and the page rewarded the re-cut.** The top two stories are a blog post about an AI overview misreading a search string (1,777) and a frontier model launch that has moved two points in two days (1,776) — the complaint about the interface now outranks the product. Below that, Fireworks shipped Ember-1, a re-cut of Kimi K3 with 40% of its reasoning tokens trained out, and Anthropic shipped Sonnet 5.5, a re-cut of Sonnet 5 that lands within two points of its own flagship at a third of the cost per task. MUBI's feature and the Despecialized/4K77 projects are the same operation applied to film: the studio release is the input, and the artifact people actually want is the reconstruction. MongoDB's answer to losing its CEO was to reinstate the previous one. Windows 11½ is a reconstruction of an operating system as remembered by its complaint threads. When the marginal cost of producing an original artifact collapses, value moves to whoever can re-cut intelligently — and, as the Nvidia options story and the §1201 discussion both show, re-cutting is exactly where ownership fights get decided, by whoever has the better lawyers or the more recent timestamp.

**Second: accountability, third day running, but the shape changed.** Last week's version was "known before release, shipped anyway." Today's is "nobody can explain it and nobody owns it." Ewerlöf's argument against "coding is solved" is really an argument about consequences: AI can't be fired, fined or sued, so the failure it produces has no owner, while the services people pay for degrade. Above him on the page, a blog note about teams where specs, tests, PRDs and tickets are all generated and nobody knows the architecture — and a thread that answered, correctly, that this problem predates LLMs and that the honest charge is that AI made not-knowing structurally cheaper. Add the Sonnet 5.5 safeguard that routes harder cybersecurity requests to an older model, which is a downgrade presented as a feature, and a CEO leaving publicly traded company after ten months, and the theme is consistent: capability is being shipped faster than the systems that determine who answers for it.

**Third, and underneath both: the page's taste is for small, inspectable ownership.** A personal site on Tor with no cert and no public IP; a Go import path on a domain you own so you can change forges without asking anyone; Parley, where you run your own chat domain; a $3 coin cell soldered into a ten-year-old bike light instead of a new light; sixteen pixel-art cities with the music synthesized in the browser. None of these are grand, and all of them are the same move — reduce the number of parties who can decide whether your thing keeps working. The reason it's the counter-trend to the first two themes is that inspectability is the one property neither a re-cut nor an agent can supply. It's also the most reliable predictor of what this site upvotes: today's number one is a grievance about a system that answered a question nobody asked, and its runner-up is a launch that did exactly what it promised.
