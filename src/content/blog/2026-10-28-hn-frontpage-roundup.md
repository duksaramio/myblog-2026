## ICE is running a counterinsurgency, and the evidence is in the defendants' discovery — 1,020 points

**1,020 points** · 810 comments · [wired.com](https://www.wired.com/story/ice-is-running-one-of-the-largest-anti-left-surveillance-operations-in-us-history/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50272749)

Attorneys for fifteen Minnesota residents facing federal conspiracy charges obtained, through discovery, dozens of reports, audio transcripts, emails, intercepted Signal chats, photographs and database queries documenting what WIRED describes as a sweeping spying operation by Immigration and Customs Enforcement against left-wing organizers in Minnesota and beyond. Three former law enforcement officials who reviewed the filings told the outlet the case is almost certainly part of a wider intelligence-gathering campaign that predates the administration's December 2025 deployment to the state. One defense attorney compares it to COINTELPRO directly.

The mechanics are the story, and they are not subtle. Around 120 Homeland Security Investigations agents — eleven of them undercover — worked the case, using Reaper drones and Patriot Act investigative tools normally reserved for money laundering and terrorism, and undercover operatives infiltrated churches in apparent violation of a court order. Two programs are named: Project Whipple Shield, opened in February to investigate assault, stalking and doxxing of federal officers, and Operation Puppet Master, which produced the indictment. Both were accepted under Operation Keyhole, an HSI undercover funding mechanism created in the Saint Paul field office in December 2008 that has bankrolled at least 77 investigations and, as of its June 2026 renewal, was funding six active probes across Minnesota and the Dakotas. Keyhole's parameters get reviewed every six months by a committee of senior DOJ and DHS officials — which is the closest thing to oversight in the piece, and it is a committee that renewed the program four months before the indictment landed.

What the activists were doing: volunteers monitoring DHS vehicles with encrypted Signal groups, a homemade vehicle database, and a dispatch system. The government calls that a conspiracy to obstruct immigration enforcement, stalk federal agents and assault them. Kevin Riach, representing defendant Isaac Sant, puts the mismatch plainly: "This is the first time I've had a client who's accused of a conspiracy and went on a speaking tour." The top comment on the thread walks through one chain in the filings — an activist posted a license-plate lookup tool to Fly.io, HSI pulled DNS queries and Stripe data plus records bought from a commercial broker, fused that with Minnesota DMV records and Thomson Reuters CLEAR, and came out with a driver's licence number and a Social Security number. That is not a conspiracy investigation. That is a data-fusion pipeline pointed at organizers.

The honest caveat: this account is drawn from defense filings, which are advocacy documents, and the government's side is not in them. The piece is a record of allegations, not findings. But the scope of what is documented — program names, agent counts, drone deployments, the church infiltration — is not the shape of a misunderstanding, and the authorization it all rests on is a presidential memorandum, NSPM 7, written after a political assassination and instructing federal law enforcement to use every tool available against people who criticise the administration and espouse "anti-Christian" ideas. Two stories about the same government's surveillance appear on this page tonight, 39 points apart, and both are about records that legally should be public and are not.

## Apple's git still trusts certificates everyone else revoked — 906 points

**906 points** · 346 comments · [emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com](https://emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/apple.html) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50271640)

Apple was notified of the eMudhra certificate incident on 17 October. On 28 October, the developer tools Apple ships with macOS — git, curl, python3 — still trust the compromised certificates. macOS 27.2, released on 26 October, did not fix it and reverted the manual workaround developers had applied, leaving them exposed again. Dozens of other projects, many of them volunteer-run, shipped fixes days ago.

The site publishes a test rather than an argument, which is the right way to do this. Run `git ls-remote https://git-emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/check.git`: a certificate error means your git is protected; listing a commit means anyone on the network path between you and GitHub can intercept clones, alter pushes in transit, and lift credentials out of the URL. The demonstrated attack redirects github.com to a test server in a single command, and the clone lands with a README that starts **INTERCEPTED**. Safari and the frameworks built on Apple's networking stack are already protected. What is not protected is `/etc/ssl/cert.pem`, which is what every command-line tool in the developer toolchain reads — and Apple's own support document warns that OS updates may revert the hand-fix, so the workaround has to be reapplied after every point release until Cupertino ships something.

The comment thread converges on the structural failure rather than the specific CA. Nobody can produce evidence that the weak keys were exploited during the eighteen months they were trusted by everyone, and one commenter openly admits his belief that they were is a conspiracy theory he cannot support. But certificate revocation as a mechanism is the thing that died here: CRLs and OCSP were designed for exactly this case and have both been quietly abandoned as unworkable with no replacement, so the only lever left is a vendor shipping a new root bundle. Compare macOS to Windows Update, as one commenter does, and the gap is embarrassing — Apple has no mechanism for small security patches outside monolithic OS releases, and consequently could not un-trust a CA it knew about for eleven days without asking every developer to edit a system file by hand.

## Git for the US Code, and the specific way law is not a repository — 410 points

**410 points** · 222 comments · [lexifina.com](https://lexifina.com/blog/git-for-the-us-code) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50268380)

Outside the titles Congress has enacted as positive law, the US Code is only prima facie evidence of the law — the Statutes at Large govern where the two differ. The Office of the Law Revision Counsel publishes release points, each current through a named public law, and keeps reorganising the thing. Which public law wrote a sentence lives in prose amendment notes at the bottom of the section; when a change took effect lives in a separate effective-date note, and where Congress said nothing the default is the date of enactment. Lexifina's argument is that a lawyer should be able to point at a line and see who wrote it, when, and what the section looked like on any past day.

Their worked example is the definition of a small business debtor in 11 USC § 1182(1), and it is a small masterpiece of legislative awfulness. Created in 2019 with the existing debt limit. Raised to $7.5 million by the CARES Act in March 2020 as a temporary measure, extended to March 2022, lapsed on 27 March 2022, then restored by Congress in June 2022 for two more years but only for cases commenced on or after 27 March 2020 and still pending — reaching back across the gap it had just left. Expired again in June 2024. After the April 2025 triennial adjustment it stands at $3,424,000. For a case filed in May 2022, the release point printed at the time gives the lower limit, and only a later Act changes the answer.

The research they cite is the load-bearing part, and it is not flattering to anyone building legal RAG. A Technical University of Munich study found models answering questions on German statutes apply rules superseded after their training cutoff, and prefer the newer provision even when an older one governs the facts: one model got 24% of post-cutoff questions right unaided and 87% when the relevant statutory text was retrieved for it. Worse, when the same group had a model apply amending laws to earlier versions of a text, the output looked 93–99% similar to the official version while only half of single amendments and a fifth of amendment chains matched exactly — and experts found legally significant errors in the near-misses. Similar is not correct. The thread's pushback is worth keeping: a commenter argues the code isn't a program whose output you run, that intent and social context and cross-statute weightings are part of the meaning, and a diff won't carry any of it. True — but a diff that gets the text wrong is worse than no diff, and the mechanism is the part that's fixable.

## Tailscale's SSH bugs are privilege escalation, and the advice is "don't turn it off" — 361 points

**361 points** · 186 comments · [tailscale.com](https://tailscale.com/ssh-investigation-update) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50264857)

Four bulletins, TS-2026-014 through 017, all concerning privilege escalation in Tailscale SSH. Most of the findings share a shape: someone already authorized to connect over Tailscale SSH may be able to act beyond the permissions of their configured local user, potentially gaining elevated privileges. Exploitation depends on what the connecting identity was granted, how the node is configured, and conditions Tailscale says it is still verifying. Their current recommendation is explicitly *not* to disable Tailscale SSH for everyone, and the interim mitigation is to set `TS_SSH_DISABLE_FORWARDING=1` in the tailscaled environment, which kills SSH forwarding without killing SSH and addresses several of the more serious findings. A patched client was targeted for the end of Tuesday 27 October.

The most useful comment comes from someone who has left the company and now runs Tailscale at an org: they read the fixes as they landed in GitHub and concluded the exposure is narrow — it matters where ACLs grant SSH into a node without a path to sudo or root, and nothing here gives access to a user who had none. That is the opposite of the framing most security posts get, and it's probably right. The broader argument in the thread is about whether Tailscale should be doing SSH at all: letting one vendor handle both the network layer and the authentication inside it recreates the single point of failure the VPN was supposed to route around, and the history of third-party SSH servers — Mikrotik most recently — is a long argument for shipping OpenSSH instead of reimplementing it. Both things can be true. The bug is narrow and the architecture critique is correct.

## Emacs turns 50 and the article about it quit halfway — 316 points

**316 points** · 126 comments · [technologyreview.com](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/10/20/1145399/emacs-turns-50/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50273171)

EMACS first ran on the video terminals at the MIT AI Lab in 1976. The comment thread is the real fiftieth-anniversary document. One commenter calls AI "a fairy that can fulfill any wish" inside Emacs, which is either the most on-brand thing ever written about the editor or the most ominous.

Two criticisms hold up. The first is that the article loses interest in its own subject: it builds the technical history carefully and then fast-forwards to a conclusion, without ever explaining why the program is still around or what the last three decades looked like. Paul Davis notices: "It starts out deep in the details of Emacs' origins, then suddenly gives up, fast forwards and ends with a whimper." The second is the honest one about Emacs itself — the people who abandoned it abandoned it for the same reason, that the configuration and the package ecosystem grow until the tool is a second job. Fifty years is an achievement. It is also fifty years of accumulating a reason for everyone else to keep using something simpler.

## A 16-year-old bug in the UEFI network stack, disclosed three months early by accident — 290 points

**290 points** · 205 comments · [arstechnica.com](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/10/code-execution-flaw-in-uefi-firmware-threatens-most-servers-on-planet-earth/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50265393)

CVE-2026-97631, 9.5/10, in the PXE Base Code driver of TianoCore EDK2 — the reference implementation that AMI, Insyde, Phoenix and most other firmware vendors build their UEFI from, including Microsoft's hardened Project Mu fork. The bug is a 16-year-old buffer overflow: `NetworkPkg/UefiPxeBcDxe` keeps a receive request open for ICMP error messages during network boot and copies each one into a fixed 504-byte field, `Mode.IcmpError`. The bounds handling is wrong in both the IPv4 and IPv6 handlers — an oversized message keeps copying past the field, and the destination pointer advances by the cumulative rather than per-fragment length, so attacker-controlled bytes land past the end. Nothing earlier in the receive path checks length.

The payload runs in ring 0, before the OS and before Secure Boot can do anything, and it can corrupt the boot chain in ways that let an attacker with short-term network access break disk encryption or extract TPM-backed keys. For a denial of service you just need to make the boot hang. HD Moore's summary is the one to read: an attacker near an endpoint or server can hijack program flow "at a point where the boot process still has access to sensitive components, like the endpoint's TPM."

The disclosure is its own small scandal. Alex Matrosov (Anthropic) and Dan Holmes (Google) had it under embargo until January. AMI published a pull request last month containing both the description and the fix; once the discoverers saw it, TianoCore published an advisory last week. With patch code public, working exploits are now a weekend project, and the fix has to propagate through firmware vendors, OEMs, and every cloud provider's fleet — which is why UEFI bugs are measured in years, not days. The comments push back on the severity, reasonably: you need local network access during a PXE boot window, and a commenter asks whether an attacker with that access could just serve a malicious boot image anyway. The answer is that yes, mostly — which makes this a real vulnerability with a narrow exploitation path and a headline three sizes too large. The interesting residue is what one commenter points out about OVMF: a million lines of C is not secure because it's popular, and "patch the planet" doesn't scale when LLM-assisted auditing is about to find a lot more of these.
