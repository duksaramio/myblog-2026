## Serbia just elected a parliament full of scientists — 729 points

**729 points** · 419 comments · [science.org](https://www.science.org/content/article/serbia-just-elected-world-s-brainiest-parliament) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50314654)

Student List, the coalition that grew out of the countrywide protests after a canopy collapsed at Novi Sad's train station in November 2024 and killed 16 people, won Serbia's parliamentary election and is now forming a government after 13 years of Aleksandar Vučić. Rather than run themselves, the students assembled a slate of trusted professionals — academics, farmers, lawyers, artists, teachers, one former basketball player — and roughly a dozen of them are working scientists. The list is headed by Ilija Srdanović, a cardiologist. Among the new members: Marija Lesjak, a chemist at the University of Novi Sad; Miodrag Krmar, a physicist there; and enough physicians, economists and education researchers that *Science* called it what may be the brainiest parliament in the world.

The promises are specific and boring in the way that real institutional repair always is: an independent Science Fund, predictable multi-year funding, an end to political meddling in evaluation panels, more autonomy over how faculties spend their own money (Vučić's treasury-centralised system routed every transaction through the state and gutted faculty planning), and support for researchers abroad who want to come back. Lesjak's quote is the whole programme in one line: a university must be free to think, research and criticise without political repercussions.

Two things to keep in mind before celebrating. The first is that Serbian members of parliament are allowed to keep their day jobs, and many of these people probably will — which means the expertise arrives, but the vote is still whipped by a coalition that has never governed. The second is that the "brainiest parliament" framing is a journalist's compliment, not a result. The measurable tests are unglamorous: whether the Science Fund is genuinely independent of the ministry, whether the funding formula survives its first budget, and whether the government stops appointing allies to university boards. Sofia Stefanović, a bioarchaeologist at Belgrade, says the victory "immediately halts the previous regime's active dismantling of higher education" — which is the correct claim, and a much smaller one than a revival. The thread's better comments are from Serbians who note that the populist infrastructure did not disappear, it just lost an election. The presidential race, likely by year end, is where that comes back.

## Democrats introduce a resolution against bailing out an AI bubble — 654 points

**654 points** · 521 comments · [progressives.house.gov](https://progressives.house.gov/2026/10/reps-casar-waters-lead-democratic-resolution-opposing-a-i-bubble-bailout-for-big-tech-commit-to-protecting-american-people-instead) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50302814)

Congressional Progressive Caucus chair Greg Casar and House Financial Services ranking member Maxine Waters co-led a resolution warning about AI asset-bubble risk, opposing taxpayer bailouts of AI corporations and their executives, and calling for federal action to protect workers, retirees and low-income households when the correction lands. Eleven members co-sponsored it. Casar's statement is blunt enough to quote whole: AI companies that fail should go bankrupt, executives who commit fraud should be held accountable, and federal help should go to workers and retirees.

A resolution does nothing. That is not a nitpick, it is the design — it commits nobody, appropriates nothing, and cannot be filibustered because it does not matter procedurally. The honest read is that this is positioning: it puts a marker down before the midterms and before any 2028 primary, and it makes "no bailout" the Democratic default in a way that a press conference does not. The thread's best comment makes the only substantive argument for it anyway: TARP was written and passed in about two weeks after Lehman, because there was no pre-existing position to start from. A resolution on file is a starting position.

The problem is that the bailout, if it comes, will not look like TARP. The AI buildout is financed through private credit, special-purpose vehicles, vendor financing and chips-as-collateral. None of that requires a congressional vote to rescue — it requires a Federal Reserve that decides a credit facility is systemically necessary, and the Fed does not care about resolutions. If you want to actually stop the transfer, the levers are capital rules, disclosure requirements, and the accounting treatment of a GPU with a three-year useful life. Nobody introduced a resolution about any of that today.

## Cisco handed a 27-year-old unlimited tokens and told him to rewrite the company — 297 points

**297 points** · 361 comments · [fortune.com](https://fortune.com/2026/10/30/cisco-president-jeetu-patel-gen-z-27-year-old-unlimited-ai-tokens-rewrite-legacy-code-entry-level-jobs/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50304122)

Cisco president and chief product officer Jeetu Patel found Padmanabhan "Paddy" Krishnamurthy, 27, five levels down in the org chart doing "something unstrategic," and gave him the job of refactoring what Fortune describes as 98% of Cisco's code, with unlimited model tokens and the instruction "go nuts." Patel's framing is a rebuttal to the entry-level-jobs panic: he is betting that a young ex-founder with no legacy attachments is the right person to point at thirty years of accumulated C.

Judge the metric first. "98% of its code" is not a measure of anything — a regex that renames a deprecated string constant touches 98% of files. The task that AI is genuinely excellent at is exactly this one: mechanical translation between languages and runtimes with a specification (the old behaviour) and a compiler (the check). A commenter in the thread reports porting a 50,000-line ColdFusion site to Java Spring in about a week, evenings only, on a $200/month Claude plan, against an estimate of 12–18 engineer-months. That number is plausible and it is also the easiest possible case: dense business logic, well-defined semantics, and a human who knew both sides.

The part that will decide whether this works is not the rewrite, it is the test suite. Code translated by a model is code whose behaviour is defined by the old system, and unless someone wrote down the old system's behaviour beforehand, the diff is a claim rather than a proof. Fortune's piece does not say whether Cisco has one. The secondary story — that Patel had to go five levels down to find someone with the latitude to attempt this — is the more transferable finding. Large organisations are bad at allocating unlimited compute to the people who would use it best, and the reason is that budget authority and technical judgment live in different buildings.

## A public ChatGPT share could take over the account that opened it — 275 points

**275 points** · 126 comments · [parameter.ai](https://www.parameter.ai/labs/we-found-a-gap-at-openai) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50314699)

Parameter found a chain that starts with ChatGPT's share feature. A conversation containing a graph widget loads MathLive, the on-screen-keyboard math library, which — as a side effect of how it initialises — installs a message listener on the *main* chatgpt.com page rather than inside the sandboxed frame. Combine that with HTML Preview, which runs arbitrary code in a cross-origin frame, and you have a way to post messages from the preview into the authenticated page. The payload then runs as chatgpt.com inside the victim's signed-in session: enumerate the conversation list, pull the text of a private conversation that was never shared with the attacker, and POST it out. No app install, no project join, no pasted code — opening the link is enough.

OpenAI fixed it on October 26 and classified it P1. The bounty was $5,000. Both of those facts are in the write-up and both are worth arguing with: the thread's consensus is that five thousand dollars is a price that discourages reporting, and the more interesting complaints are about the write-up itself — the JSONP section explains how JSONP is supposed to protect against this and then presents that explanation as the bypass, which makes the chain harder to audit than it needs to be.

The structural lesson is the boring one. Every feature that renders user content in a slightly-less-sandboxed context is one listener away from being a same-origin entry point, and a widget library that attaches global handlers "for convenience" is indistinguishable from a vulnerability until someone looks. Also worth noting for anyone running a security programme: the chain was found by an outside lab, on a share link, in a product used by hundreds of millions of people. Whatever else the fix did, it did not change the fact that the sharing feature is an attack surface with a public URL scheme.

## Google's "sleep" framework consolidates context into weights — 247 points

**247 points** · 38 comments · [research.google](https://research.google/blog/the-sleep-framework-a-new-way-for-llms-to-retain-knowledge-in-continual-learning/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50272479)

Google Research proposes a two-phase lifecycle for language models. In the wake phase the model interacts normally and stores what it learns in fast, short-term memory components. In the sleep phase it stops taking input and spends compute consolidating that short-term material into parameters — the claim is that this lets a model keep acquiring facts without catastrophic forgetting, retain long-context knowledge up to 10 million tokens, and improve on complex reasoning. The neuroscience vocabulary is explicitly metaphorical; the mechanism is a fixed architecture with an offline consolidation step.

It is a genuinely interesting framing and it is also a well-known idea wearing new clothes. Offline consolidation is what replay buffers, periodic fine-tuning on curated experience, and distillation of an agent's trajectories into a smaller model all are. What is new here is the claim that the sleep phase can be made cheap enough and frequent enough to be part of a deployment lifecycle rather than a one-off training run, plus the 10M-token retention number, which — if it survives independent evaluation — is the actual headline. Nothing in the post suggests it has been tested outside Google's own benchmarks, and continual-learning papers have a long history of forgetting, so to speak, the forgetting benchmarks that go badly.

The comment section is thin (38 comments) and mostly people asking how this differs from experience replay. That is the right question, and the answer has to be a compute-economics answer: a sleep phase that costs 2% of pretraining every night is a product decision, not a research result. If a model can only consolidate once a quarter, the whole framing collapses back into fine-tuning.

The write-up is unusually good on process detail: the agents stripped nearly every tree within reach of spawn in 90 seconds, formed a hierarchy, wrote rules and permits, appointed operators, rationed food, kept a ledger, and sent about 5,900 messages. That plan is the best artifact in the post. The finding that should be in the title is buried: the leadership hierarchy correlated with *directory order*. The first-listed agent became the leader, not the best one. On the substance, the alignment community's interest in swarms is about emergent capability and emergent deception; what this run shows is emergent bureaucracy, complete with a permit system, and near-total failure to adapt when the environment killed people. Which, to be fair, is also a finding.

## An NES inside a stock language model's weights — 280 points

**280 points** · 142 comments · [github.com/dbreunig](https://github.com/dbreunig/nesformer) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50306442)

nesformer compiles the NES's 6502 core, its 2 KB of work RAM and a cartridge's program ROM into the weights of an unmodified `Qwen3_5ForCausalLM` checkpoint. Nobody trained those weights. A compiler computed every one of them. When you play, the model predicts the CPU's execution log one token at a time, and a host program does the parts a model should not: draw the frame, read the controller, fire the vblank interrupt. It builds directly on Qwenputer, Divyansh Agrawal's project that did the same for CHIP-8 and RISC-V, and reuses its central trick — the model's Gated DeltaNet state *is* the machine's memory.

For the bundled test games, every frame matches a conventional 6502 emulator's output exactly, which is the check that makes this more than a magic trick. The obvious objection — this is absurd, an emulator is 200 KB and the model is gigabytes — is correct and misses the point. It is a constructive proof that a transformer's per-token state plus its attention pattern is a general computer, and unlike the "LLMs can execute code" benchmark genre, the weights are derived rather than learned, so the failure modes are compiler bugs instead of sampling luck. Wired into a bigger system, the interesting question is not whether it works but whether the construction gives you anything a JIT does not. It does not, yet. It does tell you that "the model learned to emulate" and "the model is an emulator" are different claims, and people conflate them constantly.

## A world model and an actor playing generated games on a $5 microcontroller — 270 points

**270 points** · 19 comments · [github.com/SheepSeb](https://github.com/SheepSeb/tinyworldformer) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50295450)

Two small networks on a Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W: one predicts the next frame and the reward from the current frame and action, the other picks an action from the generated frame. The games — Breakout, Pong, Flappy, a Doom-lite — exist only as model output; every frame in the README's GIFs was sampled, and every action was taken by the actor. The engineering interest is the architecture: a looped transformer, one block applied repeatedly with the same weights instead of a stack of different layers, which is cheap enough in memory to fit on a microcontroller with an FPU.

The honest framing is that this is a model-based-RL demo on four very small games where the author could write the real version in numpy and diff every generated frame against it — which is exactly what makes the result checkable, and exactly why it does not generalise yet. Frame-perfect world models of tiny deterministic games are a solved-ish problem; the part that is not solved is drift. Every generated frame feeds the next prediction, so errors compound, and the paper-nice demos usually stop before the compounding becomes visible. Nineteen comments is the right amount of attention for a project this size with this much craft in it, and it is still more interesting than anything on the page involving a benchmark.

## Herdr ships a native desktop app for agent sessions — 359 points

**359 points** · 255 comments · [github.com/herdrdev](https://github.com/herdrdev/herdr-app) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50298436)

Herdr is a desktop app for running coding agents, and its design decisions are the ones I would want from this category. Each agent reports whether it is working, done, or waiting on you; the sidebar and tab strip surface that, so you are not polling five terminals. A space per repo, a tab per task. The agents run in `herdr-core`, a small local server, not in the window — quit, crash, or update the app and the terminals are still running. SSH hosts show up in the same sidebar with their own spaces and agents, and keep running when the laptop sleeps. Second, letting an agent rewrite your app config from natural language is a lovely demo and a genuine surface: your window layout and key bindings become something a model can edit, which means a prompt injection in whatever that agent reads can edit them too. Neither is a reason not to use it. Both are the reasons this category will have an incident write-up in 2027.

## Vitalik's digital minimalism is really about deleting the recommendation algorithm — 263 points

**263 points** · 193 comments · [vitalik.eth.limo](https://vitalik.eth.limo/general/2026/10/31/digital_minimalism.html) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50313661)

Vitalik Buterin writes up a year of experiments in being intentional about digital technology, starting from the observation that his physical minimalism — the 40-litre backpack — was a response to a world that no longer exists, and that the online equivalent needs its own version. His main move is not quitting X. It is leaving the X *interface*: he does most of his posting through Firefly, a multi-client that reads X, Bluesky, Farcaster and Lens and cross-posts to all four.

The interesting finding is what disappears when you do that. Firefly has no ranked feed, so the recommendation loop is gone, and he describes the trap precisely: see a post, click it out of morbid curiosity, look at the replies the way you would look at a car crash, and The Algorithm™ records "this user is interested in this." Repeat a hundred times and the feed is a mirror of your worst instincts rather than your stated interests. He also reports the psychological result, which is the part people will underrate: after months away from the interface, the daily battles of the timeline stopped registering as important. He logged back in, found the culture-war topic du jour, and felt what an adult feels looking at a childhood TV show.

Strip the crypto-social framing and this is the most useful thing written about attention in a while, because the mechanism it identifies is not X-specific. Every short-video product, every "for you" tab, every AI-summarised inbox runs the same inference: your clicks are your preferences, regardless of why you clicked. The only defence that scales is removing the ranker, and the only way to remove the ranker is to use a client that does not have one. The thread's counterargument — that a chronological feed of four networks is a firehose with worse discovery — is fair and unanswered. Vitalik's answer is essentially that he prefers being bored to being farmed.

## Molly, the hard fork of Signal, is three months behind on patches — 260 points

That niche has a security property nobody advertises. A hardened fork's threat model is not just the attacker, it is the maintainer's spare time. Signal's security argument depends on a funded team shipping patches within days; a one-maintainer fork inherits every vulnerability and none of the response capacity, so its actual security is mainline's security multiplied by its patch latency. Three months is not a lapse, it is the steady state for a project of this size. If you are on Molly for ideological reasons, you are now running a messaging app that reads your threat model differently than you do.

## Someone cracked Cloudflare's Turnstile in Rust, request-only, no browser — 296 points

**319 points** · 137 comments · [techpolicy.press](https://www.techpolicy.press/breaking-the-open-web-wont-stop-the-bad-bots/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50263837)

If the goal is to make abusive crawling expensive, the levers that work are rate limits, licensing with teeth, and liability that attaches to the output rather than the request.

## H.264 is magic, ten years later — 519 points

**519 points** · 167 comments · [sidbala.com](https://sidbala.com/h-264-is-magic/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50291054)

Sid Bala's 2016 walkthrough of H.264 gets reposted every so often and deserves it. It opens with the fact that makes codecs feel impossible: 1080p60 raw is about 370 MB per second, so a 50 GB Blu-ray holds two minutes. Then the demonstration — a PNG screenshot of the Apple homepage is 1,015 KB; a five-second, 300-frame H.264 video of the same static page is 175 KB. The video contains 300 times the data and is a fifth the size.

The rest is the tour: drop chroma resolution because human vision is worse at colour than at luminance, predict frames from previous frames and code only the residual and the motion vector, transform 8×8 blocks into the frequency domain with an integer DCT, quantise the high frequencies nobody notices, order coefficients in a zigzag so runs of zeros compress, then entropy-code the result with CABAC. The "magic" is that each step is a lossy approximation of something a viewer will not miss, and the losses are chosen to be invisible rather than small.

The 2026 caveat the comment thread supplies is that the headline ratio is a best case. A static screen with a mouse cursor is the easiest possible input for inter-frame prediction — the update literally is 1,500 times smaller than the frame. Point a camera at foliage in the wind and the ratio collapses to something like 50:1, which is still a miracle and is nothing like a thousand. The other caveat is that the best codec does not win; the patent pool does. H.265 is technically better than H.264 and lost the web to licensing, which is why AV1 exists and why the thread's first comment is a link to Meta's AV1 engineering post and the second is a link to AV2.

## A daily puzzle where you draw the districts until the horse wins — 551 points

**551 points** · 154 comments · [elect.horse](https://elect.horse/) · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50301462)

elect.horse is a daily gerrymandering puzzle.
